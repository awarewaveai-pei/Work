# Western Painters 1–29｜Greatly Expanded Visual Features

> Purpose: This document only strengthens the **Visual Features** section.  
> It can be pasted directly back into each artist’s original “Visual Features” field, or used inside the style-description section of an image-generation prompt.  
> This file contains only Western painters 1–29. It does not include Eastern / Asian painters.

---

## Batch 1｜Renaissance, Baroque, and Early Fantastic Painting

### 1. Da Vinci

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should present a highly rational, precise, and quiet classical order. The figure or subject should not attract attention through exaggerated action, but should instead build mystery through a stable pose, subtle expression, and nuanced gaze. The composition may use a stable triangle, pyramidal figure arrangement, or balanced central composition, giving the image a calm, upright, immovable classical weight.

Contours should not be overly sharp. They should gradually dissolve into the background through soft transitions of light and shadow. The face, hands, skin, fabric, and background edges should all have a sfumato-like smoky blending effect, allowing forms to appear as if they are naturally emerging from the air. Facial expressions should be restrained, inward, and contemplative. Avoid broad smiles, crying, shouting, or theatrical open mouths. The eyes may carry psychological depth, like a silent riddle the viewer cannot fully decode.

The background should include atmospheric perspective and mist-softened distance. Faraway landscapes, architecture, or natural scenery should gradually decrease in contrast layer by layer, forming a soft sense of spatial depth. Lighting should mainly come from gentle side light. Avoid harsh spotlights and modern photographic hard shadows. The overall surface should feel like a classical oil painting: refined, transparent in its layers, smoothly blended, and combining scientific observation, human proportion, natural study, and quiet mystery.

---

### 2. Michelangelo

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The core of the image should be sculptural human power. Figures should not merely stand or sit; they should feel driven by immense spiritual force, showing twisting, stretching, rising, resisting, supporting, or explosive poses. Muscles should be clear, heavy, and weighty. Even in stillness, the body should feel as if it contains the tension of a marble statue.

The composition can lean toward fresco-like, ceiling-painting-like, or epic staging. Figure proportions may be slightly heroic. Shoulders, back, arms, thighs, and torso should have strong structural presence. Gestures should include contrapposto, torso rotation, raised arms, bending, upward gaze, falling movement, or ascension. The image should not feel weak, floating, or delicately decorative; it should present strength, sublimity, religious gravity, and sculptural anatomy.

Light and shadow should behave like light falling on sculpture, emphasizing bodily volume, bone structure, and muscular transitions. Drapery can also feel heavy and powerful, like carved stone. The background may include ceiling-fresco space, clouds, mythological space, altars, monumental architecture, or religious scenes, but the human body must remain the visual center. The overall effect should be monumental, solemn, and spiritually overwhelming, as if exceeding ordinary human scale.

---

### 3. Raphael

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should pursue harmony, balance, clarity, and idealized classical beauty. Figures should have naturally flowing poses, neither stiff nor overly theatrical. Faces should be dignified, gentle, compassionate, and ideally proportioned, with emotional stability. The composition may use triangular structures, symmetry, circular groupings, or a clear central arrangement so that the image feels stable, luminous, and free of chaos.

Interactions between figures should be elegant and restrained. Gestures, gazes, head tilts, and body directions should form a gentle rhythm. Every figure should seem placed within perfect order without becoming mechanical. Madonnas, children, philosophers, classical figures, or sacred groups can all be rendered as soft, noble presences.

The space should be bright, clean, and supported by classical architecture or open background depth. Perspective should be clear but not showy. Lighting should be even and soft, allowing figures to emerge naturally from the background. The overall surface should be smooth and refined, with gentle color transitions. The image should possess sacred calm, idealized beauty, and academic classical order rather than darkness, roughness, or excessive emotion.

---

### 4. Botticelli

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should have poetic, linear, decorative, and early Renaissance flat beauty. Figures may be elongated, graceful, and slightly unreal. The body does not need heavy sculptural volume; instead, beauty should arise from flowing outlines, elegant poses, rhythmic drapery, and delicate contour. Female figures, mythological subjects, springtime, flowers, sea breeze, long hair, and light fabrics are especially suitable.

Line is the core. Hair, drapery, leaves, fingers, and facial contours should be drawn with clear yet gentle curves. The image may resemble a mythological stage, a classical poem, or a decorative tapestry, without requiring realistic spatial depth. The background may be slightly flattened, with flowers, woods, shoreline, or classical architecture acting as rhythmic ornament rather than realistic scenery.

Facial expressions are usually quiet, elegant, and slightly melancholic or distant. Avoid modern sensuality, exaggerated expressions, or heavy oil-paint chiaroscuro. The overall image should present a gentle, poetic, mythological, linear, and decorative classical beauty, like a mythic poem transformed into painting.

---

### 5. Caravaggio

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image must have a strong tenebrism effect. Large areas of the background should sink into deep black or dark brown, while figures are suddenly illuminated by a single strong light source, creating theatrical impact. The light should enter diagonally from outside the image, striking the face, hands, shoulders, fabric, or the core of the event, while the rest of the space is swallowed by darkness.

Figures should feel real, rough, and physically weighty rather than overly idealized. Faces may show wrinkles, dirt, fatigue, tension, or shock. Fingers, skin, clothing, wounds, and objects should have tangible realism. The composition should often be close to the viewer, with figures cropped against the edge of the frame so the event feels as if it is happening directly in front of us.

Actions should capture dramatic instants: reaching, falling, pointing, revelation, judgment, attack, mourning, or a moment of shock under sudden illumination. Avoid even lighting, gentle pastoral beauty, or clean commercial polish. The whole image should feel like a real human event on a dark stage: rough, sacred, violent, revelatory, tense, and inescapable.

---

### 6. Rembrandt

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should let human spirit slowly emerge from deep darkness. Unlike Caravaggio’s sudden theatrical light, Rembrandt-like light should be warmer and more introspective, like candlelight, hearth glow, or golden-brown light inside an old theater. The face should be the core: light concentrates on the forehead, bridge of the nose, cheek, eyes, hands, or collar, giving the figure a sense of age, memory, and spiritual weight.

Brushwork should have thickness and visible touch. Highlights may use impasto, with slightly raised paint in the lit areas; shadows should be deep, transparent, and built in layered glazing. Skin should not be overly smooth. It can show wrinkles, spots, fatigue, looseness, and roughness. Fabric, metal, fur, hats, and background should also be handled with loose but precise brushwork.

The composition may be a portrait, elder figure, scholar, biblical character, family scene, or contemplative figure. The pose does not need to be exaggerated, but it must have psychological depth. The whole image should feel quiet, heavy, warm, introspective, and humane, as if preserving the trace of a life inside darkness.

---

### 7. Vermeer

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should feel like a quiet interior where time has been suspended. The composition is usually stable, clean, and precise. Figures are engaged in ordinary actions, such as reading, pouring milk, writing a letter, looking out a window, arranging objects, or quietly focusing on something small. Gestures should be restrained and never emotionally violent. The emphasis is on air, light, silence, and the stable relationships between objects.

Light often enters from a window on the left, gently falling across walls, faces, fabrics, tabletops, ceramics, maps, glass, or pearls. Highlights should be small, clean, and pearl-like. Edges are soft but object structure remains clear. Perspective is precise, and the geometry of the interior is evident. Checkered floors, wall maps, window frames, chairs, tablecloths, and ceramic jugs can serve as typical elements.

Color and light should feel clear, never heavily dark or modern-photographic sharp. The figure’s expression should be silent and focused, like a low-voiced moment of daily life. The image should have a clean, restrained, delicate, milk-soft light quality and the quiet atmosphere characteristic of Dutch interior painting.

---

### 8. Velázquez

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should carry the cool dignity of Spanish court portraiture. Figures should stand or pose naturally, not overly sweetened or idealized. Nobles, attendants, painters, children, and court figures can all carry distance, authority, and silent presence. The composition may include dark interiors, mirrors, pictures-within-pictures, the viewer’s gaze, and complex spatial relationships.

Brushwork is essential: from a distance the image feels realistic, but up close the handling is loose, fast, and free. Fabrics, lace, ribbons, gold and silver ornaments, hair, and background do not need to be rendered stroke by stroke; they can be suggested through masterful patches of tone and brushwork. Faces and hands may be more precise, while clothing and space remain painterly.

Lighting is usually subdued, silvery-gray, and cool rather than theatrically bright. The color palette should feel restrained, dignified, and courtly. A subtle relationship between the figure and the viewer can turn the image into more than a portrait: it becomes an elevated painting about power, vision, and space.

---

### 9. Goya

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should carry unease, satire, shadow, and the darkness of human nature. Figures may be court nobles, crowds, war victims, grotesque beings, nocturnal apparitions, or allegorical characters, but they should all feel caught between reality and nightmare. Expressions may be fearful, numb, absurd, exhausted, or frenzied, but avoid polished horror illustration.

Brushwork may be rough, loose, and scratched. Dark areas should carry weight. Backgrounds can be oppressive, empty, or blurred, making figures seem swallowed by history, violence, or psychological shadow. In the direction of the late Black Paintings, large areas of black-brown background, sickly skin tones, low light, and unsettling gazes may be used.

The composition can feel unstable. Proportions and expressions may be slightly strange, but should retain real-world weight. Avoid ornate gothic fantasy or clean fantasy art. The total effect should be a rough oil painting where political allegory, war memory, absurd human nature, and psychological fear converge.

---

### 10. Bosch

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should resemble a highly dense encyclopedia of religious fantasy. The composition may use the feeling of a triptych, juxtaposing paradise, earthly life, and hell, or may unfold a vast narrative through layered distance. The image should be filled with tiny figures, monsters, hybrid creatures, strange tools, symbolic objects, architectural fragments, instruments, punishments, temptations, nude figures, and moral-allegorical scenes.

The density of detail must be very high, with miniature events in every corner. Monsters can combine birds, fish, insects, human bodies, vessels, egg-shaped structures, machines, musical instruments, and architecture. Avoid a single monster or a single subject; there must be multiple layers of narrative and many symbols, making the image feel like a collection of riddles, dreams, and religious warnings.

The space does not need to be fully rational, but it should still follow a medieval pictorial order. Paradise areas can be bright and translucent; hell areas may mix firelight, smoke, and darkness. The overall image should not look like modern fantasy-game art. It should feel ancient, allegorical, eerie, intricate, and morally judgmental.

## Batch 2｜Romanticism, Impressionism, and Post-Impressionism

### 11. Turner

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should be swallowed by light, mist, storm, and atmosphere. Object contours do not need to be clear. Ships, buildings, coastlines, flames, or figures can be half-dissolved in luminous fog. The goal is not precise object description, but the feeling of wind, moisture, steam, waves, clouds, and light moving violently.

The composition may use swirling clouds, diagonal storms, explosive sunlight, surging water, or fire and smoke. Bright areas may spread outward like a golden core of sun, flame, or light through mist; dark areas should be engulfed by smoke-gray, seawater, dark brown, or blue-gray vapor. Sky and water may bleed into one another until the horizon becomes unclear.

Do not make the image overly sharp, clean, or photographic. Brushwork should feel flowing, swept, diffused, wet, and atmospheric. The overall image should present the Romantic sublime: nature is not a background, but a vast, unstable force that overwhelms humanity.

---

### 12. Monet

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should focus on momentary light and color rather than fixed outlines. Trees, flowers, water, buildings, figures, or sky should be broken apart by soft natural light into short brushstrokes and shimmering color. Contours can be loose; edges should not be too sharp. Objects should feel as if they are gently vibrating in the air.

Water reflections, gardens, water lilies, grass, mist-covered buildings, riverbanks, afternoon sunlight, and morning haze are especially suitable. Shadows should not use pure black, but should be expressed through blue-violet, gray-green, pale pink, cool complementary color relationships. The image should contain outdoor air, light penetration, and moist reflection.

Brushstrokes should be visible and lively: short strokes, broken marks, and juxtaposed color patches forming an overall luminous effect. The composition can be calm, but the surface must be alive. The result should be bright, fresh, soft, and flowing, like nature being changed by light in a specific instant.

---

### 13. Manet

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should have a modern urban sensibility and a cool directness. Figures may face the viewer frontally, with expressions that are lucid, detached, and slightly provocative, without classical idealization or sweet poses. The composition may include photographic cropping, flattened backgrounds, large tonal areas, and strong black-white contrast.

Brushwork should be looser and bolder than academic painting. Color areas are clean, without excessive blending. Black can act as a major visual force, used in clothing, hats, backgrounds, or contours. Figures and background may be slightly flattened against one another, making the painting feel modern, direct, and deliberately anti-academic.

Scenes may include bars, interiors, urban women, social spaces, modern clothing, tabletop still lifes, or street fragments. Avoid excessive romance, soft-focus prettiness, or theatrical drama. The overall effect should be nineteenth-century modern life suddenly entering oil painting: cool, clear, elegant, detached, and slightly challenging to the viewer.

---

### 14. Renoir

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should be filled with warmth, softness, pleasure in life, and dappled sunlight. Figures are usually rounded, gentle, and intimate. Skin should carry warm light, not sharpness or cold hardness. Garden gatherings, dances, intimate interactions, women, children, friends, afternoon leisure, and tender daily life are all suitable.

Light may seem to pass through leaves, scattering flickering patches over people. Edges between fabric, skin, flowers, and background can soften, with loose and sweet brushwork. Poses should feel natural, with smiles, conversations, turning bodies, dancing, seated positions, or closeness between figures.

Colors should be warm and soft, mixing pink, peach, cream white, pale blue, grass green, and golden yellow. Avoid cold gloom, rough expressionism, or photographic realism. The image should feel as if sunlight, skin, gardens, and social joy have fused together through soft oil-paint brushwork.

---

### 15. Degas

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should feel like an observed fragment of urban daily life, not a formal staged pose. The composition may use diagonal angles, off-center placement, partial cropping, figures cut off by the frame, or suddenly shifted space. Ballet dancers, rehearsal rooms, backstage spaces, bathers, horse racing, theater observation, and urban interiors are especially suitable.

The human action must be accurate. Figures may stretch, bend, adjust shoes, lean on a wall, wait, turn, sit tiredly, or perform rehearsal gestures. Bodies do not need to look perfectly elegant; they can show work, practice, fatigue, and everyday movement. Line, posture, and spatial arrangement matter more than romantic beautification.

The material can have dry pastel grain or loose oil brushwork. Colors are often soft but gray-toned, with powdery edges. Light may come from stage lighting, interiors, or side illumination; it should not become a glamorous theatrical still. The overall image should carry an observer’s viewpoint, a sense of instantaneity, structure, and cool capture of modern life.

---

### 16. Cézanne

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should transform nature into stable geometric structure. Apples, tabletops, mountains, trees, houses, figures, or bathers should not be treated only as appearances, but as if built from cylinders, spheres, cones, planes, and color blocks. Objects need weight, architectural presence, and inner order.

Brushwork may use short, firm, parallel marks, with layers of color blocks. Do not pursue smooth realism. Tabletops may tilt slightly, space may contain multiple viewpoints, and object edges may not align perfectly, creating rational but subtly unstable structure. Avoid simple photographic perspective and excessive softness.

Colors should feel solid and grounded. Ochre, earth green, blue-gray, brick red, cream, and deep blue can build form together. The image should look simple but sturdy, as if every object slowly grows weight within the painting. This style is suitable for making a subject rational, structural, solid, and proto-Cubist.

---

### 17. Seurat

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should be made of dense, regular small dots or short color marks. From a distance, they form complete figures and landscapes; up close, they reveal the granular structure of optical color mixing. No object should be filled with a large smooth area of color; light, shade, and color should be created through the juxtaposition of blue, green, yellow, orange, pink, violet, and other tiny marks.

The composition should be stable, ordered, and almost stage-like. Figures may stand still, appear in profile, and keep quiet distance from one another in riverside, park, lawn, seaside, or afternoon scenes. Motion should not be overly intense; the image should have a calm, rational, slightly detached feeling of public space.

Contours may be clear, but interiors should still be built from color dots. Shadows should not be gray-black smears; they should be made from complementary color points that create cool-warm vibration. The overall effect should be bright, scientific, precise, and still, as if Impressionist light were organized into a rational system.

---

### 18. Van Gogh

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image must have strong directional brushwork and thick paint. Sky, trees, roads, wheat fields, stars, flowers, portraits, or rooms should appear driven by inner emotion, showing rotation, burning, vibration, or flow. Brushstrokes must not be smooth. They should be short, visible, rhythmic, and forceful, so the viewer can feel the hand’s energy across the surface.

Contours may be heavy and dark. Objects may show subjective deformation. Cypress trees can resemble flames; skies can become vortexes; starlight can form burning rings; wheat fields can move like waves. Portraits should also carry psychological unease, loneliness, and intensity, rather than merely realistic faces.

Color should be highly saturated and high-contrast, especially strong collisions between blue and yellow, green and orange, ochre and deep blue. The overall emotion should be passionate, lonely, poetic, and unsettled, as if nature has been transformed into spiritual energy. Avoid clean digital brushes or generic impasto; the surface should feel touchable, with oil paint buildup and directional strokes.

---

### 19. Gauguin

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should have symbolism, flattening, and decorative structure. Space does not need natural perspective. Figures, plants, land, sky, and background may be compressed into large areas of color. Contours should be clear, often enclosing forms with dark lines, creating a cloisonnist, enamel-like effect.

Figures should be quiet, mysterious, and ritualized, not modern or theatrical. Tropical plants, island figures, mythic scenes, religious suggestions, and dreamlike narratives are suitable. Backgrounds may use non-natural colors so that color carries psychological and symbolic meaning rather than realistic light.

Color blocks should be intense without depending on realistic shadows. Rust red, violet, peacock green, deep blue, earth yellow, orange, and black contour can build an exotic, mysterious, dreamlike atmosphere. The overall image should feel like reality transformed into a primitive, decorative, symbolic spiritual scene, not a tourist-style tropical landscape.

---

### 20. Munch

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should externalize psychological states through line, color, and spatial pressure. Faces may be elongated, simplified, and distorted. Eyes may feel empty, fearful, lonely, or numb. Bodies can be thin and shadow-like, echoing the wave-like lines of the background.

The background should not merely be scenery; it should feel like inner vibration. The sky can twist into blood red, orange-red, deep blue, or gray-violet waves. Bridges, cities, coastlines, hospital rooms, interiors, or roads may press toward the figure. Lines should be emotional, as if anxiety is spreading through the air.

The composition may use an isolated figure, oppressive foreground, distant crowds, strong diagonals, or enclosed spaces. Color must directly serve anxiety, death, loneliness, and existential unease. Do not turn it into a simple horror film image or pretty gothic illustration. The overall image should feel like psychological screaming, emotional shock waves, and modern loneliness made visible.

## Batch 3｜Modernism, Abstraction, and Pop Art

### 21. Klimt

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should combine realistic figures with flat decoration. The face, hands, shoulders, or exposed skin can be relatively delicately realistic, but clothing, background, and surrounding space should be covered with gold leaf, geometric patterns, flowers, spirals, eye-like shapes, rectangles, dots, mosaics, and jewel-like motifs. The figure should seem surrounded by ornate decorative imagery, even partially merging into a decorative plane.

The composition may be vertical, portrait-like, icon-like, or intimate and embracing. Poses should be sensual, soft, and mysterious; expressions should be quiet and dreamlike. Avoid excessive action or generic fashion illustration. Decorative patterns should feel symbolically meaningful, not random.

Gold is central. It may feel like gold leaf, bronze, jewelry, or the surface of an icon. The total image should exist between luxury and mystery: both flat and sumptuous, both portrait and decorative wall surface. It should carry the refinement, sensuality, and symbolic atmosphere of the Vienna Secession.

---

### 22. Mucha

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should resemble a refined Art Nouveau poster. The figure is usually placed centrally or within a symmetrical composition, with graceful posture, elegant hands, flowing long hair, and drapery that can curve like vines. The surrounding space should include flowers, plants, circular halos, arched frames, vines, borders, decorative title panels, or theatrical poster structures.

Lines should be clean and delicate, with clear contours. Colors should be flat and soft, not dependent on strong realistic shadows. The face should be gentle and dignified, not a modern anime face or a photographic model. Decoration is more important than deep perspective, and the overall image should carry a vintage print quality.

The composition should pay attention to borders and rhythm. Floral motifs and curved ornaments can occupy the top, bottom, left, and right, but should not become chaotic. Pale gold, cream, powder green, soft pink, lavender, pale blue, and brown linework should create a gentle, romantic, elegant quality between commercial poster and decorative art.

---

### 23. Matisse

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

Color and decorative space should be the main characters of the image. Figures, plants, windows, tabletops, vases, interiors, textiles, and landscapes can all be simplified into large flat color areas. Forms do not need realistic proportions; they should retain a bright, free, joyful rhythm.

Lines may be fluid and simple. Black contours or dark lines can separate color fields. The background should not merely be background; it should be filled with decorative patterns such as floral wallpaper, grids, plants, carpets, window views, and tablecloths, making the whole space feel like a flat collage. Perspective can be compressed, and front-back relationships do not need to be fully realistic.

Color should be highly saturated, direct, and bold. Bright red, cobalt blue, emerald green, vivid yellow, orange, and pink can create strong complementary contrasts. The image should feel light, bright, and musical rather than dark, realistic, or heavy. It should look as if color itself is dancing, rather than objects being illuminated.

---

### 24. Picasso

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should present Cubist multi-viewpoint decomposition and reconstruction. Figures or objects should not be shown from a single correct perspective; instead, front view, side view, top view, and partial cross-section may appear simultaneously. Faces may have displaced features, asymmetrical eyes, a profile nose overlapping with a frontal mouth, and bodies broken into geometric planes before being reassembled.

Space should be fractured, flattened, and collage-like. Boundaries between subject and background may interpenetrate. Still lifes may include musical instruments, bottles, tabletops, newspapers, chairs, windows, and geometric fragments. Portraits should show deformation, analysis, and rational dismantling. Avoid generic abstract scribbles; the image must retain structure, plane relationships, and multi-viewpoint logic.

Color may follow Analytic Cubism with ochre, gray, black, cream, brown, and dark blue, or use stronger later blocks of red, yellow, and green. The whole image should have intellectual, experimental, modernist tension and broken space, rather than natural realism or traditional perspective.

---

### 25. Dalí

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should present absurd dreams through precise realistic technique. Space is often clear, dry, and empty, with a distant horizon, desert, isolated objects, long shadows, and cold hard light. Objects may be irrational, but their edges, textures, cast shadows, and details should be very clear, creating the effect of “impossible things painted as if real.”

Elements may include melting objects, floating forms, distorted bodies, drawers, eggs, insects, bones, broken statues, vast spaces, double images, or impossible perspective. Objects do not need real-world logic, but they must have dream logic: calm, eerie, and strongly symbolic.

Lighting can resemble harsh noon sun or cold stage light, creating sharp long shadows. Do not make the image blurry, soft, or vaguely dreamlike. Dalí-like Surrealism needs clarity, precision, dryness, silence, and unease. The viewer should feel the image is absurd, yet every object is rendered almost too realistically.

---

### 26. Miró

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should be made of floating symbols, thin black lines, organic shapes, dots, stars, eyes, bird-like forms, moons, suns, and simplified creatures. Do not pursue realistic space. Symbols should dance, float, and respond to one another inside broad areas of pure color. Negative space is important; the image should not become a generic pattern field.

Lines should feel spontaneous and light, like children’s drawing, but with precise composition. Symbols may seem naive, yet their arrangement needs rhythm and cosmic feeling. Forms can remain ambiguous, between person, bird, insect, celestial body, and abstract sign. The image should feel like a dream, fairy tale, cosmic map, or poetic symbol system.

Colors may use basic red, blue, yellow, black, white, and green in a clean, direct way. The background can be a large pure color field or pale open space. The overall image should feel naive, free, poetic, and cosmic, but not chaotic or cheap cartoonish. It should be minimal yet lively modern abstract poetry.

---

### 27. Kandinsky

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should feel like a visual symphony. Points, lines, planes, circles, triangles, arcs, diagonals, color blocks, and irregular forms should collide, echo, float, or cross through each other, forming a musical rhythm. It should not depict specific objects; it should depict energy, sound, spiritual motion, and inner tension.

The composition may include diagonal movement, central explosion, circular gathering, geometric balance, or free lines crossing the image. An early direction may be more expressive, colorful, and fluid; a Bauhaus direction may be more geometric, ordered, and rational. In either case, the image must have abstract rhythm and spirituality, not a random pile of shapes.

Colors should have clear contrast. Red, yellow, blue, black, white, violet, orange, and green can act like different musical tones interacting. The overall image should have dynamic balance, abstraction, musicality, and spiritual symbolism, as if the color structure could be heard.

---

### 28. Mondrian

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should be extremely simplified, made only of vertical lines, horizontal lines, rectangles, white space, and a small number of primary-color blocks. No curves, no natural objects, no shadows, no gradients, and no hand-painted texture. All visual force should come from the precise balance of black grid lines and rectangular proportions.

The composition should be asymmetrical but stable. Black line thickness can be uniform or slightly varied, but must create architectural order. Red, yellow, and blue blocks should not be overused; they should act as key points in the image’s balance. White space is not emptiness, but a structural element as important as the colored blocks.

The image should feel clean, rational, pure, modern, and architectural. Any natural light, material shadow, brushwork, figure, landscape, or ornament would break the style. The whole image should feel like the world reduced to its most basic order and balance.

---

### 29. Warhol

**Visual Features — Greatly Expanded Version**

The image should present the mechanical reproduction and commercial printing quality of Pop Art. The subject may be a celebrity portrait, product packaging, canned goods, logos, consumer objects, or media imagery, treated through repeated arrangement, flat color blocks, coarse screen dots, halftone, silkscreen misregistration, and high-contrast color.

The image can include color misalignment, printing flaws, unnatural color overlays, shifted outlines, and traces of mechanical reproduction. Human faces or products do not need natural color; bright pink, fluorescent yellow, cyan, orange, vivid red, purple, and black can create artificial, advertising-like, commodified visual impact.

The composition may use a single large portrait, four-panel grid, repeated multi-panel layout, horizontal repetition, or poster-like design. Do not pursue traditional oil brushwork or psychological depth. The image should feel like something reproduced, consumed, and commodified by mass media. The overall effect should be bright, flat, mechanical, coolly humorous, commercialized, and instantly recognizable as pop culture.
